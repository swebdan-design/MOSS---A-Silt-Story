# THE FIRST LISTENER
## Notes on MOSS, Childhood and the Sound of a Place

*MOSS — A SILT Story · Synth Centric*

Most of us have a place we visited as children and can no longer quite find.
The house is still there, perhaps, and the road still bends where it bent.
What we cannot find is the attention we brought to it. At ten, an afternoon
had no edges. A fallen log was a drum and a door and a small dark room where
something might live. We did not know we were listening. We simply had not
yet learned which sounds to ignore.

MOSS begins in that kind of place: a grandmother's cottage, a village with
four houses and a well, a wood, a creek, a railway line that no longer has
trains, a hill. It is the last summer. Everyone says so, as if the summer had
been told. The boy has come to help his grandmother pack and, without meaning
to, he spends the visit learning how to hear.

### Hearing almost nothing

The game is built so that, at first, very little happens in the ear. A few
notes, sparse, like someone testing a piano in another room. This is not an
absence of design. It is how a new place sounds to someone who has not yet
stopped to listen to it. The world is full of sound and almost none of it has
reached him.

Then he drops a pebble into a small pond he does not remember, and the pond
gives back a note. Something in the reeds answers. From that moment the boy's
holiday has a question in it, and the question is musical.

Each of the eight things he learns to hear — wind in the reeds, water over
stones, insects in long grass, the hollow of a fallen log, rain on a wooden
roof, a bell answered by a cutting, stillness, and memory — is a real change
to the pond and to the world. The reeds become playable. Water gives the pond
a low voice that moves slowly under everything. The insects begin to answer
notes, and, being insects, they leave space for each other. Rain arrives as
small circles. Echo lets a note come back from the far side of the water. None
of this is announced as a reward. The boy simply hears more, and so do you.

### A sound among other sounds

There is an idea in acoustic ecology that every living place has a kind of
order to its sound: that creatures occupy different heights and times of the
soundscape so they can be heard, and that a healthy place is not quiet but
arranged. Crickets do not shout over each other. The wind and the water are
the ground everyone else stands on. Human sound is one voice among them, often
the loudest, rarely the wisest.

MOSS borrows this gently. The insects in the pond will not answer a note that
sits in the high register the melody is using. The water's drone moves only
every half-minute or so, the way a landscape moves. The music is never just
the player's. A note you place is heard in the company of everything else the
pond is already doing.

This is the inheritance from SILT. In the first SILT, a little pond on a
Nintendo DS, you placed lilies and two stones sent out circles; music happened
where a circle met a lily. The listener set up the conditions and the pond
performed within them. MOSS keeps those two stones, the same eight-by-five
pond and the same patient ripples, and asks where that pond might have come
from — or rather, who first noticed it.

### Small machines

The Game Boy has four voices. Two are square waves, one plays a tiny
hand-drawn waveform of thirty-two steps, and one makes noise. There is no
reverb, no sampling, no room to hide. Every sound in MOSS is one of those four
voices doing something simple: a pulse that decays like a struck bell, a thin
trill that starts and stops like a cricket, a rounded hum that slides slowly to
a new note, a soft burst of noise for a raindrop.

Limits like these are often treated as a problem to be overcome. Here they are
the instrument. A child's attention is also a small machine. It cannot hold
everything, so it chooses; and what it chooses becomes the place. Four voices
are enough for a pond in the same way that a few remembered sounds are enough
for a summer.

### Returning

Returning to a place we knew as children is strange because the place has not
waited for us. The grandmother has grown older. The station has lost its
trains. The gardener's beans come up whether or not anyone is there to see
them. Mr Hollis, the gardener, says it best: seasons don't end, they hand over.

The boy is learning the same thing from the other direction. He thought he
was discovering the pond. He was, in a sense, only arriving at it. It had
been there for his mother as a girl, and for whoever came before her, and it
will be there for Iris, who promises to look at it after he leaves. Looking,
she says, is a kind of looking after.

There is a phrase that the station bell plays — low, then high, a fifth —
which turns up again in his grandmother's wind chime, and again, if he
remembers to ask, in the pond. His grandfather hung the chime because it
played the bell's two notes; when it rang he would say the wind was coming
from the railway, long after the trains had stopped. It is a small thing, a
private joke kept up for years. It is also how memory works. A sound outlives
what made it. Someone keeps saying the old words. A child hears them and
doesn't know yet that he is now one of the people who will remember.

### Listening without intervening

The pond in MOSS can be played. You can drop pebbles, place lilies, choose
scales and weather, and make something that sounds like your own. It also
learns, quietly: the intervals you favour, the rhythms of your hands, the
kinds of sounds you return to. Over time it will lean a little toward you. It
will not become you.

But the most important control is the one that does nothing. Press SELECT and
the cursor and the words disappear. There is only the water. The stones keep
their time, the insects keep their manners, the light rain keeps falling if it
was falling, and the pond goes on without instruction. One of the things the
boy learns in the clearing is that at first he heard nothing, and then he
heard everything. Both were true. The difference was him.

### The pond remains

At the end of the story the boy goes to the pond one last time. He sits. The
pond plays something it could not have played on the first day — something
made from the garden he left in it and the habits it learned from him. He
leaves a small stone on the bank, not for the pond, which does not need
anything, but so that something of his gets to stay. Then he walks away, and
the game does not follow him.

The music continues. That is the whole of the ending, and it is meant to be
both sad and not. A summer cannot be kept. A place can be loved without being
owned. The listener leaves, and the sound does not stop, because it was never
waiting for us in the first place. We were simply lucky to be there while it
played.

The SILT releases that followed begin with a pond at dusk and two stones,
and someone sitting down beside the water. They were always a little
mysterious about who had been there before. MOSS does not answer that. It only
suggests that every pond has a first listener, and that the first listener is
never really the first.
